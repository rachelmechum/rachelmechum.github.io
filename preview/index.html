Research

I study binary black hole mergers with gravitational wave data and population models.

What is a waveform and what does RIFT do?

I’m studying how the waveform model we use changes the black hole properties we infer from a merger.

A gravitational waveform is a computer model of the signal from a black hole merger. We simulate possible waveforms and compare them with signals detected by LIGO. That comparison helps us estimate the black holes’ properties like their masses and spins. Click here to read how LIGO explains grav waves.

RIFT or Rapid Iterative FiTting is the software I use to compare detected signals with these simulated waveforms. It helps estimate the black holes’ properties. I use it to study how those estimates change with different waveform models. That matters a lot for binaries where the black holes have very different masses.

Why are AGN disks interesting for black hole detections?

I’m studying whether black holes in AGN disks and embedded star clusters can form pairs that merge and how often we might detect those mergers.

An active galactic nucleus (AGN) is a bright galaxy center powered by a supermassive black hole. The gas around it piles into a swirling disk, and that disk is a busy place. Smaller black holes can get caught in it and dragged around with everything else. Some of them bump into each other, pair up, and eventually merge. Each merger sends gravitational waves rippling out toward our detectors.

I use McFACTS to simulate many possible black hole pairs in these environments. The results help predict which mergers detectors might see. Comparing those predictions with observed mergers can help test whether AGN disks are one place black hole pairs form.

Black holes in comic form

The Black Hole Comics Project is a free comic book about black holes and gravitational waves, developed by physicist Rodrigo Panosso Macedo in collaboration with six artists.

Each artist brings a different style to the science. The stories explore how black holes form, spacetime curvature, and the history of Karl Schwarzschild (who derived the first exact black hole solution). The story even ends with a nod to LISA, our future space-based GW detector!

You can learn more and download the book at blackholecomicbook.com.

Statistical machine learning for astronomy

I came across Yuan-Sen Ting’s Statistical Machine Learning for Astronomy through a LinkedIn post and wanted to share it here.

It starts with probability and statistics, then works up to things like Gaussian processes and neural networks, explaining the math along the way. It also gets into how to account for uncertainty in astronomical data. There are 22 Jupyter notebook tutorials so you can try things out with real data.

I’ve only just discovered it, but this seems like something I wish I had a few years ago!

You can read it online, download the textbook, or browse the tutorial notebooks.
